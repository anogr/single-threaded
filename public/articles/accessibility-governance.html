Accessibility Governance

How organizations turn accessibility targets into ownership, delivery practices, procurement decisions, evaluation, and ongoing improvement.

Andreas Ögren
~10 min read
Last updated August 2026

An audit can identify barriers in a product, but the report does not explain who must prevent the next regression. Teams need a shared target, people who own the decisions behind it, evidence from the right kinds of evaluation, and a way to keep those arrangements working as the product changes.

Accessibility governance is the system an organization uses to assign that responsibility and verify the result. It covers product policy, design and engineering practices, content publishing, procurement, testing, user feedback, and reporting. Governance is not a separate approval layer that makes a product accessible after implementation. It gives each existing delivery activity an accessibility responsibility.

The work starts with the barrier model described in Web Accessibility. A design system can prevent repeated semantic defects, while a product team still owns the complete workflow. A procurement process can reject an inaccessible service before integration, while operations still needs a route for users to report barriers that escaped evaluation. Each boundary needs an owner because no single specialist can control the whole experience.

Standards and Conformance

WCAG supplies a technical baseline, while laws, contracts, and organizational policy decide which baseline applies.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are the W3C standard for accessible web content. WCAG 2.2 is the latest WCAG 2 Recommendation and was approved in October 2025. Its success criteria are organized into levels A, AA, and AAA, with each level including the levels below it. W3C encourages teams to use the latest WCAG 2 version, but WCAG 2.2 does not deprecate WCAG 2.1 or 2.0. A governing rule may still name one of those earlier versions.

Level AA is a common target because it includes the foundational Level A criteria and a wider set of barriers that products are generally expected to address. Level AAA contains additional requirements that can improve access for particular content and audiences. W3C advises against requiring Level AAA for an entire site because some content cannot satisfy every AAA criterion. Teams can still adopt relevant AAA criteria where the user need and product context justify them.

A conformance claim applies to complete pages and processes, not isolated components. A component library can test a field's semantics, keyboard operation, and error API. Only the product can determine whether a multi-step application process preserves instructions, focus, error recovery, and accessible third-party content from beginning to end. Component evidence lowers risk without certifying the page that consumes it.

Regulation and Contracts

Accessibility law creates obligations, but the applicable jurisdiction and product scope determine what a team must implement.

Both the European Union and the United States regulate digital accessibility. In the EU, common requirements for selected products and services began applying in June 2025. In the US, federal rules cover public-sector digital services, while disability law also applies to businesses open to the public. State law, sector regulation, and public funding can add further obligations.

A law does not always provide the checklist that a designer or developer follows. It may define which organizations and services must be accessible, then refer to a technical standard or leave the organization to choose suitable criteria. WCAG is often used for that technical baseline, but the required version, conformance level, exceptions, and evidence depend on the governing rule.

Contracts and procurement policies can be more specific than legislation. A customer may require a particular WCAG target, evaluation report, remediation period, or process for reporting regressions. Legal specialists determine which obligations apply. Product and architecture teams turn those obligations into scope, acceptance criteria, evidence, and ongoing ownership.

Choosing a Target

A target becomes useful only after the organization connects it to product scope and delivery decisions.

When no governing rule requires a different version, WCAG 2.2 Level AA is a sensible product target. The policy should identify the websites, applications, documents, and third-party content it covers. It should also state supported languages and platforms, how legacy content will be handled, when conformance will be evaluated, and where exceptions require documented approval.

Product teams then translate the target into acceptance criteria for complete workflows. A requirement such as “meet WCAG 2.2 AA” is too broad for a feature ticket on its own. A checkout feature can specify keyboard operation, visible focus, field labels, error relationships, status announcements, reflow, and the behavior of its payment provider. Those conditions give design, implementation, and review a common definition of finished.

Accessibility targets also need resources. Training, expert review, assistive-technology testing, disabled-user research, remediation, and supplier evaluation take time and money. A policy without an allocated budget transfers those costs to individual teams and makes delivery depend on local enthusiasm.

Distributed Responsibility

Every role owns the barriers created by the decisions it controls.

Product leadership owns scope, priority, budget, and the consequences of releasing known barriers. Researchers include disabled participants and the assistive technologies or adaptive strategies relevant to the product. Designers specify content order, interaction, focus, motion, contrast, responsive behavior, and states that static default screens often omit.

Content authors own headings, link purpose, text alternatives, captions, instructions, and clear error wording. Developers preserve semantics and implement keyboard, focus, state, and announcement behavior. Design-system teams encode safe defaults and document the responsibilities that remain with component consumers. Quality engineers exercise workflows and transitions rather than scanning only settled pages.

Procurement owns accessibility requirements for products the organization does not build. Operations and support own the path through which users report a barrier and receive an accessible alternative while it is corrected. An accessibility specialist can train teams, advise on difficult patterns, and evaluate evidence. The specialist cannot substitute for these owners because most decisions occur before a central review can see them.

Systems and Content

Shared systems can prevent repeated defects, but they cannot decide whether a finished page communicates clearly.

The design system can supply native controls, visible focus treatments, approved color pairings, reduced-motion utilities, and documented keyboard models. Components should be evaluated at their source and again in representative product contexts. Tokens narrow unsafe choices but cannot prove contrast, focus visibility, or reflow after a page combines colors, layers, sticky regions, and content.

Component APIs need explicit boundaries. A field component can require a visible label and provide an error-message relationship. The product still writes the label, chooses when validation occurs, and decides whether the message helps a user recover. A modal component can provide dialog semantics and focus containment, while the product chooses a useful name, initial focus, and the destination when the dialog closes.

Publishing tools create another shared boundary. A content management system can require alternative-text decisions, preserve heading structure, and produce valid markup. It should not force every image to have descriptive text because decorative images need a different result. Templates, editorial guidance, validation, and preview tools work together to help authors make the contextual decision.

Third-Party Products

A supplier becomes part of the user journey, so its accessibility belongs in architecture and acceptance.

A payment flow, identity provider, embedded support tool, document viewer, or analytics widget can introduce barriers into an otherwise accessible application. Procurement should include the applicable standard, product and platform scope, evaluation expectations, remediation terms, disclosure of known gaps, and notice when an update changes accessibility behavior.

The Voluntary Product Accessibility Template (VPAT) is a template for producing an Accessibility Conformance Report (ACR). A completed report identifies the product version, evaluation methods, applicable standards, and the supplier's account of how the product supports each criterion. It is evidence supplied by the vendor, not a certification.

Review the report for specific explanations, current product scope, and testing that matches the planned environment. Vague remarks, an old version, or blanket claims without methods reduce its value. Representative workflows still need evaluation before purchase and after important upgrades. Where a supplier has gaps, the contract needs an accepted workaround, remediation commitment, or a decision not to use the product.

Runtime Overlays

A script that modifies a finished page cannot establish that its content and workflows conform to WCAG.

An overlay widget is a third-party script that attempts to alter an existing site at runtime, often through a control displayed in a corner of the page. It may change presentation or patch some detectable defects. It cannot determine whether text alternatives fit their context, repair an inaccessible business process, rewrite unclear instructions, or verify behavior across the browser and assistive-technology combinations a product supports.

The limitation is the same one that applies to automated evaluation: tools cannot determine every accessibility requirement without human judgment. In 2025, the US Federal Trade Commission finalized an order requiring accessiBe to pay one million dollars over deceptive claims that its automated product could make any website WCAG-compliant.

An overlay cannot establish conformance

Treat an overlay like any other third-party script with a narrow, testable purpose. It cannot replace accessible source code, workflow evaluation, expert judgment, or testing with disabled users.

Evaluation and User Research

Different evaluation methods find different kinds of evidence, so a credible program combines them.

Automated rules can quickly detect mechanical defects such as missing attributes, invalid relationships, and some contrast failures. They work well in component development, continuous integration, and repeated page scans. A passing scan does not establish conformance because many requirements depend on purpose, reading order, interaction, timing, or the quality of supplied text.

Manual evaluation examines keyboard operation, focus order and visibility, zoom and reflow, text alternatives, errors, dynamic updates, and complete task behavior. Assistive-technology testing checks the browser contract that users actually receive. Test combinations should reflect product analytics, audience research, language, and supported platforms; results from one screen reader and browser cannot be generalized to every combination.

Disabled-user research provides evidence about usability and barriers that standards-based checks may not reveal. It should happen during research, design, and iterative delivery rather than only after implementation. A small participant group cannot represent every disability or establish WCAG conformance, so user involvement complements systematic evaluation instead of replacing it.

Independent expert audits remain useful at major milestones, for regulated evidence, and when internal teams need a broader assessment. Findings should enter the ordinary issue tracker with an owner, severity, affected users, target release, and regression test where one is possible. A report that remains outside the delivery system documents risk without controlling it. Testing Strategy explains how these layers fit into the wider test portfolio.

Sustaining Accessibility

Accessibility changes with the product, its dependencies, its content, and the standards that govern it.

Monitor representative workflows after release, not only individual URLs. Track regressions, unresolved barriers, supplier gaps, audit findings, user feedback, and the time taken to provide an accessible alternative. Trend information is more useful than a single score because it shows whether teams are preventing defects or accumulating them.

Users need a clear way to report a barrier and receive a response. Support staff need enough context to recognize an accessibility problem, avoid asking a user to repeat an inaccessible process, and route the issue to an owner. Feedback can reveal combinations and tasks that planned testing missed.

Review the governance system when products, platforms, suppliers, standards, or laws change. Update training and shared components when the same defect recurs across teams. Accessibility becomes sustainable when the organization can name the target, the owner of each decision, the evidence required before release, and the route from a reported barrier to a verified correction.
