Data and IP in AI Tooling

Where code goes when an assistant reads it, what the vendor may do with it, and who owns what comes back.

Andreas Ögren
~11 min read
Last updated July 2026

Before adopting an AI development tool, an organization needs to know where the provider processes submitted code and how long it stores the content. The governing terms should explain whether the provider may use submissions for training and which claims it will cover if generated output infringes another party's rights. Copyright ownership follows a separate legal test.

The purchased product, deployment, and signed terms determine how submitted content is handled. The deployment assigns operational responsibility, and the contract states what each provider may do with the data. Governing AI-Written Code explains how teams review generated output. The data and intellectual-property (IP) terms require a separate analysis.

A product name is too coarse for this review. The editor extension may collect repository context, send it through a vendor gateway, route the request to a separate model provider, and store task state in another service. The organization needs the complete data flow for the plan and features it will enable. A policy based only on the model host can miss the systems that select, log, or cache the content around inference.

Deployment Models

A deployment model determines where inference runs and divides control and operational work between the organization and its providers.

A hosted service sends content outside the developer's machine for inference. The provider may receive the prompt and open files along with repository context or tool output gathered during the task. Because an agent explores the repository, it can transmit more material than a short chat request. Procurement must identify every receiving party and record where each party processes the content and what it may do with it.

Consumer software as a service (SaaS) includes free and individual tiers of hosted tools. These plans are governed by standard consumer terms and individual account settings. The provider can change the available settings or defaults under those terms. The organization has no negotiated contract unless it reaches a separate agreement with the provider.

Enterprise SaaS adds organization-level terms and controls. A data processing agreement (DPA) can define the customer's and provider's roles, identify subprocessors, and set retention and security obligations. It can govern whether customer content may be used to train models. Managed workspaces put administrative policy into practice, while identity integration and audit logs help administrators control and trace access. The word “enterprise” alone does not specify which commitments the vendor provides.

A managed model service runs through the organization's cloud account. Existing identity and network controls may extend to the service, along with established monitoring and regional arrangements. Product terms determine how the service processes and retains data. Under the shared-responsibility model, the provider operates the service while the customer is responsible for its configuration and application behavior. The customer must classify the data and control who can access it.

A self-hosted open-weight model runs in infrastructure the organization controls. Data can be kept inside the chosen boundary when telemetry, connected tools, and external dependencies do not send content elsewhere. The organization operates the hardware and model-serving stack and takes responsibility for security updates, monitoring, and model provenance. Organizations may choose this deployment when their data requirements justify the additional operational work.

The four models differ along several dimensions, so they do not form a single scale from weak to strong control. Enterprise SaaS may provide firmer contractual commitments than a managed service purchased under standard cloud terms. Self-hosting gives the organization direct control over infrastructure but makes it responsible for more security and reliability work. A useful comparison covers processing location, retention and training terms, network egress, administrative controls, and operational responsibility.

One coding session can cross more than one deployment boundary. A locally running agent may send model requests to a hosted API, download documentation from the internet, and call a remote issue tracker. Local execution controls the agent process and files, but it does not make those remote calls local. Reviewers should trace each source of data to every destination rather than assign one label to the whole product.

Retention and Training

A provider may store submitted content without using it to train model weights.

Retention describes how long a provider stores inputs, outputs, or derived application state. Training describes whether submitted content may be used to improve model weights or related systems. The two settings vary independently, so a provider can retain content for abuse monitoring without training on it. Zero-data-retention controls can remove eligible content from provider logs, but eligibility and application-state exceptions must be checked for each endpoint.

Storage can occur at several layers. Request logs may hold prompts and responses for abuse detection. A coding product may keep conversation history, uploaded files, indexes, or cached tool results as application state. Feedback submitted by a user may enter a separate improvement pipeline. A statement about API log retention may cover only the first layer, so the review should ask about every stored copy and its deletion path.

Training terms need equal precision. A provider may exclude customer content from general model training while using it for a customer-specific fine-tune, evaluation, safety analysis, or product analytics under separate terms. The relevant question is which permitted purpose applies to each data class. Broad phrases such as service improvement need a contractual definition when sensitive source code enters the service.

Consumer products commonly express these choices through account settings and standard terms. Enterprise and API products more often provide organization controls and contractual exclusions, although the details differ. Cloud-hosted model services have their own product terms. With self-hosting, the organization decides how its systems log requests and delete stored content. It controls whether collected data is used for fine-tuning.

A default worth checking

GitHub states that it may use interactions from individual Copilot plans for AI training unless the user opts out. The change took effect on April 24, 2026. Business and Enterprise data is covered by different terms. Anthropic lets consumer users choose whether chats and coding sessions may be used for improvement. It may keep selected data in a de-identified form for up to five years in its model-training pipelines. Because these examples are time-sensitive, approval should depend on the current plan and settings as well as the endpoint and contract.

A negotiated term binds the provider, while an administrative setting configures the purchased product. Procurement should record the contractual commitment beside the setting that activates or supplements it. A later review must compare both because product defaults and configurations can change after the DPA is signed.

Secrets Hygiene

Provider terms cannot protect a secret after an agent has sent it outside the approved boundary.

Repositories and working directories often contain material that should never enter a model request. An exploring agent may reach environment or local configuration files, logs, and scripts containing credentials. If a hosted agent reads a secret, a promise not to train on customer data does not undo the disclosure. The boundary must prevent the read or redact the content before transmission.

Enforceable file-access and ignore rules should protect paths that may contain secrets. Secret scanning and short-lived credentials reduce the effect of a missed rule. A context file can explain the boundary, while a sandbox or policy can prevent the agent from crossing it. The organization's data-flow threat model should include prompts and tool results as channels that may carry sensitive data.

An instruction that says not to read .env helps the model choose its actions but does not enforce the boundary. File permissions, sandbox rules, and tool allowlists can prevent the read. Network policy can restrict where an agent sends data after reading it. Short-lived, narrowly scoped credentials reduce the damage if prevention and detection both fail.

Secrets can arrive through tool output even when the repository is clean. A diagnostic command may print an authorization header, and an issue description may contain a copied token. Redaction should therefore cover command output and connected services as well as files. When a credential reaches an unapproved provider, rotation is the reliable response because deletion requests cannot prove that every copy or log entry has disappeared.

Copyright in Generated Code

The deployment location does not determine whether generated code receives copyright protection.

Copyrightability follows a different analysis from provider data handling. A contract can allocate rights between the customer and vendor. Copyright law determines whether the generated material can receive protection in the first place, so deployment does not settle authorship.

The US Copyright Office's current guidance requires sufficient human authorship. Purely machine-generated material receives no copyright protection under this guidance. Human selection, arrangement, or creative modification may qualify case by case. Other jurisdictions may apply different rules, and the guidance does not provide a code-specific ownership formula. Legal review is appropriate when ownership affects a product or transaction.

Real diffs often mix generated material with human choices, edits, and integration work. The attribution record can preserve that provenance, although a log does not itself establish copyright. Counsel and future maintainers can use the record to see how the work was produced.

Three questions often appear in one procurement discussion but need separate answers. The provider contract can assign whatever rights the provider has in the output. Copyright law decides whether protectable authorship exists. Licenses on source material or similar output may impose conditions regardless of who owns the new file. An assignment clause therefore does not guarantee copyright protection or clear third-party rights.

Infringement Risk and Indemnity

Generated output can create an infringement claim even when the contract assigns the output to the customer.

A separate risk is that generated output may resemble protected material or carry license obligations the user did not expect. Litigation over model training and output is unsettled, and its outcome will not necessarily answer every customer's downstream liability. A repository policy may use public-code similarity detection to send matching output for license review. Independently, the organization needs a clear account of which claims the vendor contract covers.

Indemnification is a contractual promise to defend or compensate a customer for specified third-party claims. Microsoft's customer copyright commitment is one example and requires covered customers to use applicable safeguards. Each agreement defines the scope and exclusions of its protection along with any liability cap. Notice duties and required product settings may impose further conditions. A marketing summary should never be treated as the operative promise.

Coverage should be read against realistic claims. An agreement may cover copyright claims arising from unmodified output while excluding prompts that ask the service to reproduce a known work, combinations with customer material, or use after the provider sends a warning. The liability cap may be lower than the cost of a serious dispute. Procurement should record the covered products, eligible users, required settings, exclusions, and procedure for notifying the vendor.

Any technical condition attached to indemnity belongs in the deployment baseline. The organization should record and enable each required filter or guardrail and assign an owner for later setting changes. Missing a required safeguard may prevent the organization from invoking the protection it purchased.

Procurement Review

A procurement review connects data classification and enforceable terms to the product's running controls and later checks.

Procurement begins by classifying the material the tool may read, including content discovered automatically during agent work. The classification should determine the product and deployment boundary. Where the risk requires contractual protection, the agreement should identify processing locations and subprocessors and define retention and training use. Deletion behavior and incident obligations belong there as well. Each term must then map to a concrete administrative setting or operating control.

The running configuration must implement the purchased controls. The organization should enable and record filters required by an indemnity term. Connecting administrative policy and audit systems to the identity provider lets AI-tool access follow established account controls. Ignore rules and enforceable file boundaries should prevent agents from reading secrets.

Verification needs evidence that can survive staff changes. The review record can link the signed agreement and current subprocessor list to screenshots or exported settings from the managed workspace. A technical test can confirm that a blocked file is not transmitted and that deleted task history disappears from the user-facing product as documented. Provider attestations may cover infrastructure controls, while product-specific behavior still needs its own check.

The arrangement should be verified on a schedule and after material product changes. The review should compare the DPA with current defaults and endpoint retention while checking for changes to subprocessors or model providers. Indemnity conditions should be included in this review. The goal is to catch drift between the purchased promise and the running configuration before an audit or incident reveals it.
