Data and IP in AI Tooling

Where code goes when an assistant reads it, what the vendor may do with it, and who owns what comes back.

Andreas Ögren
~7 min read
Last updated July 2026

Adopting AI development tools raises four questions that the editor cannot answer. The organization needs to know where submitted code is processed, how long the provider retains it, whether it may be used for training, and what contractual protection applies to generated output. Copyright ownership is related but follows a different legal test.

Most data-handling answers depend on the product, plan, deployment, and signed terms rather than on the underlying model. This article follows those choices from consumer software to self-hosting, then separates retention from training and ownership from indemnity. Governing AI-Written Code covers the development process around the output; this article covers the data and intellectual-property (IP) terms underneath it.

The Deployment Ladder

Four rungs, and almost every guarantee changes at each one.

For a hosted service, content submitted for inference is processed outside the developer's machine. That may include the prompt, open files, retrieved repository context, and tool output. An agent can expose more material than a small chat request because it explores the repository during the task. Procurement must therefore evaluate what is transmitted, where it is processed, and what each receiving party may do with it.

The bottom rung is consumer SaaS, the free and individual tiers of the mainstream tools. Guarantees are thinnest here. Controls live in a settings page the vendor can reorganize, defaults change with product announcements, and the contract is a consumer terms-of-service the organization never negotiated.

Enterprise SaaS adds organization-level terms and controls. A data processing agreement (DPA) can define roles, subprocessors, retention, security obligations, and whether customer content may be used to train models. Administrative policy, audit logs, identity integration, and managed workspaces then help enforce the purchased terms. The exact commitments vary by vendor and contract, so “enterprise” is a product category rather than a guarantee by itself.

The third rung uses a managed model service through the organization's cloud account. Existing identity, networking, monitoring, region, and procurement arrangements may extend to that service, but the relevant product terms still determine processing and retention. The shared-responsibility model applies: the provider operates the service, while the customer remains responsible for configuration, data classification, access, and application behavior.

The fourth rung runs an open-weight model in infrastructure the organization controls. Data can remain inside the chosen boundary if telemetry, dependencies, and connected tools are configured accordingly. The organization also assumes responsibility for hardware, serving, patching, model provenance, monitoring, and updates. The choice follows from data sensitivity and operational capacity rather than from an assumption that one rung always provides the best model.

Retention and Training

Stored and trained on are different fates, and the difference is contractual.

Retention describes how long a provider stores inputs, outputs, or derived application state. Training describes whether submitted content may be used to improve model weights or related systems. The settings vary independently: a provider can retain content for abuse monitoring without training on it. Zero-data-retention controls can remove eligible content from provider logs, but eligibility and application-state exceptions must be checked per endpoint.

Consumer products commonly express these choices through account settings and standard terms. Enterprise and API products more often provide organization controls and contractual exclusions, although the details differ. Cloud-hosted model services have their own product terms, while self-hosting moves the question to the organization's own logging, fine-tuning, and data-lifecycle practices.

A default worth checking

GitHub states that, from April 24, 2026, it may use interactions from individual Copilot plans for AI training unless the user opts out; Business and Enterprise data is covered by different terms. Anthropic lets consumer users choose whether chats and coding sessions may be used for improvement, with a longer retention period for content placed in training pipelines. These examples are time-sensitive. Verify the current plan, settings, endpoint, and contract before approving a tool.

A settings toggle is useful operationally, but a negotiated term provides a different form of assurance. Procurement should record both: the contractual commitment and the configuration that activates or supplements it. Product defaults can change, so neither the signed DPA nor the admin console should be reviewed in isolation.

Secrets Hygiene

The biggest leak is often not the vendor's policy but what the tooling was handed.

Repositories and working directories often contain material that should never enter a model request. Environment files, local configuration, logs, and credential-bearing scripts may all be reachable during agent exploration. If a hosted agent reads a secret, a promise not to train on customer data does not undo the disclosure. The boundary must prevent the read or redact the content before transmission.

Use enforceable file-access and ignore rules for known secret-bearing paths, backed by secret scanning and short-lived credentials. A context file can explain the boundary, but prose is weaker than a sandbox or policy that denies access. Prompts and tool results should appear in the organization's data-flow threat model as additional channels crossing a trust boundary.

Who Owns Generated Code

The one question the deployment ladder does not answer.

Copyrightability follows a different analysis from provider data handling. A contract can allocate rights between customer and vendor, but it cannot create copyright protection for material that the law does not protect. Deployment choice therefore does not settle authorship.

The US Copyright Office's current guidance requires sufficient human authorship. Purely machine-generated material is not protected, while human selection, arrangement, or creative modification may be protected case by case. The guidance is not a code-specific ownership formula, and other jurisdictions may apply different rules. Legal review is appropriate when ownership materially affects the product or transaction.

Real diffs often mix generated material with human choices, edits, and integration work. The attribution record can preserve that provenance, although a log does not itself establish copyright. It gives counsel and future maintainers evidence about how the work was produced instead of forcing them to reconstruct the process later.

Infringement Risk and Indemnity

Not what the organization owns, but what it could be accused of copying.

A separate risk is that generated output may resemble protected material or carry license obligations the user did not expect. Litigation over model training and output remains unsettled, and its outcome will not necessarily answer every customer's downstream liability. Organizations therefore need both technical controls for matching public code and a clear account of which claims the vendor contract covers.

Indemnification is a contractual promise to defend or compensate a customer for specified third-party claims. Microsoft's customer copyright commitment is one example and requires covered customers to use applicable safeguards. The scope, exclusions, caps, notice duties, and required settings vary by agreement. A marketing summary should never be treated as the operative promise.

Any technical condition attached to indemnity belongs in the deployment baseline. Record the required filter or guardrail, verify that it remains enabled, and assign an owner when product settings change. Otherwise the organization may pay for protection while failing to satisfy the condition needed to invoke it.

The Procurement Checklist

The decisions in ladder order, made once and revisited on a schedule.

Begin by classifying the material the tool may read, including content discovered automatically during agent work. Use that classification to choose the product and deployment boundary. Record processing locations, subprocessors, retention, training use, deletion behavior, and incident obligations in the contract where the risk requires it, then map those terms to concrete administrative settings.

Then wire in what the rung provides. Turn on the filters the indemnity depends on, and record that they are on. Connect the admin, policy, and audit surface to the organization's existing identity provider, so access to AI tooling is governed like access to everything else. Extend ignore rules and context-file boundaries to keep secrets out of what agents read.

Re-verify the arrangement on a schedule and after material product changes. Review the DPA, current defaults, endpoint retention, subprocessors, model providers, and indemnity conditions together. The goal is to catch drift between the purchased promise and the running configuration before an audit or incident reveals it.
