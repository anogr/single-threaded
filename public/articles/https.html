HTTPS and Certificates

How HTTPS works: the two problems it solves, the TLS handshake, certificate anatomy, and the chain of trust behind the padlock.

Deep dive
Andreas Ögren
~9 min read
TLS & certificates
Last updated July 2026

Encryption keeps the data moving between a browser and a server private. Certificates prove that the server on the other end is actually who it claims to be. HTTPS bundles both together. Most of the confusion around it has nothing to do with encryption at all, it's the identity half that gets complicated, especially once private, company-internal services enter the picture.

This article works through both halves. It covers how the TLS handshake sets up an encrypted connection in a handful of round trips, what a certificate contains, and how browsers decide whether to trust one. It closes with where things typically go wrong once internal services and local development environments get involved.

HTTPS solves two separate problems

Symmetric and asymmetric encryption are the easier half of this story, and largely a solved problem by this point. The harder half, the one certificates exist to solve, is identity.

Solved by symmetric + asymmetric crypto
Encryption

Stop anyone on the network (a coffee-shop Wi-Fi, a misconfigured proxy, a curious neighbor on the VPN) from reading the traffic passing between client and server. This is what most people mean when they say "HTTPS."

Solved by certificates + a chain of trust
Identity

Prove that the server on the other end of a connection is actually the one it claims to be, not something impersonating it. This is the part that causes the most confusion once internal, non-public services enter the picture.

Both problems get solved in a handful of round trips, before a single byte of the page loads. Here's the sequence.

Why HTTPS matters even with nothing to hide

Browsers don't only use connection security to decide whether to show a warning icon. A long list of capabilities are gated behind what's called a secure context, regardless of whether the page handles anything sensitive. They refuse to initialize over plain HTTP. localhost gets an exemption, covered below, but a plain internal hostname over HTTP does not. This is often the real reason an internal tool needs HTTPS in development: not because it's transmitting secrets, but because a feature it depends on won't run without one.

APIs gated behind a secure context

Service Workers, the Clipboard API, camera and microphone access, Geolocation, WebAuthn, and crypto.subtle all refuse to initialize outside a secure context.

Four steps, a few milliseconds

Steps 1 and 2 use asymmetric math (ECDHE) to derive a shared secret. From there, everything including the certificate travels encrypted, and step 4 is where ordinary application traffic begins.

StepWhat happensCrypto
1. Client Hello Browser opens the connection and says hello: supported TLS versions, supported cipher suites, a random number, and an ephemeral public key share for the key exchange. Nothing secret is transmitted yet. plaintext
2. Server Hello Server picks a cipher, sends back its own random number, and its own key share. At this point both sides can independently derive the same shared secret using Diffie-Hellman math, without ever transmitting the secret itself. asymmetric (key exchange)
3. Certificate + Finished The server's certificate, its signature, and a Finished message all travel encrypted with keys derived from that shared secret. The browser checks the certificate chain against its trust store and verifies the signature, then replies with its own Finished message. certificate verification
4. Secure session Both sides now hold symmetric session keys derived from the earlier exchange. Every request and response from here on is encrypted with fast symmetric crypto. symmetric (bulk encryption)

Anatomy of a certificate

It's just a small, signed document. Each field below serves a specific purpose.

FieldExamplePurpose
Subject (SAN) app.internal.company.com The identity this certificate vouches for. Modern browsers check the SAN list specifically, not the older Common Name field, even if it's also present.
Issuer Acme Internal CA The Certificate Authority that verified this identity and signed the certificate. Could be a public CA like Let's Encrypt, or an internal CA a company runs itself.
Validity 90 days A not-before and not-after date. Expired certificates are treated as fully untrusted, there's no grace period.
Public key EC P-256 The server's public key. The matching private key never leaves the server, proving possession of it is part of how the server proves its identity.
Signature SHA-256 + RSA The issuer's signature over the rest of the certificate, made with the issuer's private key. This is the part a browser actually verifies.
How the server knows which certificate to send

The table above skips a detail: a single IP address, load balancer, or CDN edge often fronts hundreds of unrelated hostnames, each with its own certificate. The browser tells the server which one it wants through SNI (Server Name Indication), a hostname sent in plain text as part of the Client Hello, before any encryption keys exist. That's also why SNI leaks the destination hostname to anyone watching the network, even though everything after the handshake is encrypted. Encrypted Client Hello (ECH) closes that gap by encrypting SNI too, though support across browsers and middleboxes is still inconsistent.

The chain of trust

A certificate is only as good as whoever signed it. Browsers walk the chain upward until they hit a root that's already trusted.

Trust anchor
Root CA

Self-signed and kept offline or tightly protected. Its public key is preinstalled in the OS and browser's trust store.

Day-to-day issuer
Intermediate CA

Signed by the root, used for everyday certificate issuing so the root itself rarely has to be touched or exposed.

app.internal.company.com
Leaf cert

The certificate a server presents during the handshake. Signed by the intermediate.

Self-signed certificates break the chain

A self-signed certificate is signed by its own private key instead of a CA's. There's no chain to walk and no trusted root at the end of it, so the browser can't verify identity, even though the connection is still encrypted.

Certificate Transparency and revocation

The chain proves who signed a certificate. Two more mechanisms keep the signers honest and deal with certificates that have to be pulled early.

A CA is trusted to vouch for identities, but nothing in the chain itself stops one from issuing a certificate for a domain it never should have, whether through a mistake or a compromise. Certificate Transparency (CT) addresses that by requiring every publicly trusted certificate to be recorded in append-only public logs when it's issued. The CA gets back a signed timestamp proving the certificate was logged, and browsers reject a certificate that doesn't carry them. Chrome has enforced this since 2018 and Safari since 2021. The practical payoff is that a domain owner can monitor the logs and spot a certificate for their domain that they never requested, which is often the first sign a CA has been tricked or breached.

Revocation is the opposite problem: a certificate that was issued correctly but now has to be killed before it expires, because its private key leaked or the domain changed hands. The two traditional mechanisms both disappointed. A Certificate Revocation List (CRL) is a CA-published list of revoked certificates, historically too large to download on every connection. The Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP) let a browser ask the CA about a single certificate in real time. That leaked browsing history to the CA, which learned which site the user was visiting. It was also toothless: browsers treated a failed lookup as success, so an attacker could just block the check. The industry has largely moved on. Let's Encrypt shut down its OCSP responders in 2025, and the CA/Browser Forum has made OCSP optional. Browsers now rely on their own aggregated, compressed revocation sets shipped with updates (Chrome's CRLSets, Firefox's CRLite) rather than querying a CA per connection.

This is also the real reason certificate lifetimes keep shrinking. If revocation can't be trusted to pull a bad certificate out of circulation quickly, the alternative is to make certificates expire soon enough that a leaked one has little value. That is the logic behind the schedule to cut maximum validity toward 47 days, covered under the pitfalls below. A short lifetime is a revocation mechanism that always works, at the cost of making automated renewal mandatory.

Trust stores, and why internal certs get flagged

About 150 root Certificate Authorities ship preinstalled in most operating systems and browsers. Public CAs like Let's Encrypt chain up to one of these, which is why their certificates "just work" with no warning.

Companies often run their own internal certificate authority for services that were never meant to be public. Those certificates are perfectly valid technically, but they're untrusted by browsers and tools like Node.js unless that internal CA's certificate has been explicitly added to the relevant trust store. A device that's never been told to trust it treats the certificate exactly like a self-signed one: same red triangle, same "connection is not private" page.

The fix isn't a different kind of certificate. It's distribution. Once an internal root CA's public certificate is installed into the trust store on every device that needs to reach those internal services, typically pushed out once via MDM or group policy, the warnings disappear.

Node.js: a second, separate trust store

Getting a CA trusted by the operating system fixes the browser, but Node.js doesn't read that same OS trust store by default. It ships with its own bundled list of root certificates. A Node script, an SSR process, or a test runner calling that same HTTPS endpoint can still fail with UNABLE_TO_VERIFY_LEAF_SIGNATURE or self signed certificate in chain, on a machine where the browser shows no warning at all.

shell Bash 
NODE_EXTRA_CA_CERTS=/path/to/ca.pem node server.js
The tempting shortcut

NODE_TLS_REJECT_UNAUTHORIZED=0 disables certificate verification entirely rather than fixing the underlying trust problem. Fine for a five-second debug, risky to leave anywhere near a real environment.

The practical playbook

Three different situations, three different answers. Most "why is this cert broken" confusion comes from mixing these up.

Local dev (Vite): make the local machine trusted

mkcert creates a tiny local CA, installs it into the local trust store, then issues real certificates for localhost or any other local hostname, with no warnings.

shell Bash 
mkcert -install
mkcert localhost app.local
vite.config.js JavaScript 
import fs from 'node:fs'

export default {
  server: {
    https: {
      key: fs.readFileSync('./localhost-key.pem'),
      cert: fs.readFileSync('./localhost.pem'),
    },
  },
}

Quicker but noisier: @vitejs/plugin-basic-ssl auto-generates a self-signed cert, but the browser will still warn.

Shared internal environments: one CA, company-wide

Staging, QA, and internal tools shared across teams typically rely on a company-run internal CA, usually managed by a platform or IT team. That CA issues certificates for *.internal.company.com-style domains, and its root certificate gets pushed to every employee device once.

If a warning shows up on one machine but not another, it's almost always a device missing that root certificate, not a broken deployment.

Production-style services: let the edge handle it

A Node application usually shouldn't terminate TLS itself. A reverse proxy or load balancer placed in front holds the real certificate instead.

nginx.conf Nginx 
# nginx terminates TLS,
# proxies to Node in plain HTTP
listen 443 ssl;
ssl_certificate     app.crt;
ssl_certificate_key app.key;
location / {
  proxy_pass http://127.0.0.1:3000;
}

Node listens on plain HTTP behind it, inside the network boundary the proxy already secured.

Things that trip people up

PitfallWhat to know
Expiry isn't optional Let's Encrypt has issued 90-day certificates for years, and that's no longer the exception. The CA/Browser Forum's maximum validity for any publicly trusted certificate dropped from 398 to 200 days in March 2026, and is scheduled to keep shrinking to 100 days in 2027 and 47 by 2029. No exceptions for "1 second past expired" either way. Automate renewal or it breaks silently, usually at the worst time.
SAN, not CN Modern browsers ignore the old Common Name field entirely. If a hostname isn't listed in the Subject Alternative Name field, the certificate fails validation, even when the CN looks correct.
Mixed content An https page calling an http API gets the request silently blocked. Common when a Vite dev server is https but proxies to a plain-http backend.
"Untrusted" ≠ "unencrypted" A self-signed certificate still encrypts the connection fine. The warning is purely about identity verification, not about weaker encryption.
localhost gets a free pass http://localhost is treated as a secure context automatically by browsers. app.internal.company.com gets no such exemption.
Check the whole chain A server that sends only its leaf cert and forgets the intermediate will work in some browsers and fail in others, depending on what's cached.
HSTS remembers Once a browser sees a Strict-Transport-Security header for a domain, it refuses plain HTTP for that domain again for the cached duration: no warning, just an automatic redirect or a hard failure. Some entries are even browser-preloaded and effectively permanent.
Certificate Transparency Certificates from public CAs like Let's Encrypt get published to public CT logs by design. An internal-only hostname issued one of these becomes publicly searchable, even though the service behind it isn't reachable.
Wildcards aren't infinite A certificate for *.internal.company.com covers exactly one subdomain level, not deeper nesting like api.app.internal.company.com, and not the bare domain itself.

This is a high-level pass by design. For the full detail, running openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -text on any real certificate reveals every field described above.
