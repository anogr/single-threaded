Web Accessibility

How a web page preserves its meaning and operation when people perceive and control it in different ways.

Andreas Ögren
~30 min read
Last updated August 2026

Consider a person who is blind and wants to book a medical appointment. The clinic's website uses an interactive calendar to show which times can be booked. A sighted visitor can understand the calendar from its layout and color, but a blind visitor needs the browser to make the calendar available to assistive software that reads the page aloud. If the calendar exposes only a grid of unlabeled controls, the appointment exists visually but cannot be booked through the interface.

The barrier comes from the way the website represents the task, not from the person's inability to see the screen. The person may book the appointment without difficulty on another site whose controls have meaningful labels and work from a keyboard. A product becomes inaccessible when it requires an ability that a user does not have and provides no equivalent way to complete the task.

Web accessibility is the work of preventing those barriers. An accessible interface keeps a website available to people with disabilities, including when they use assistive software such as a screen reader or operate the page without a mouse. Features introduced for accessibility can also help people without disabilities, such as someone who prefers to use a keyboard or cannot play audio in their current surroundings.

How Disabled People Use the Web

A design must preserve necessary information and behavior across different ways of perceiving and controlling a page.

Screen Readers and Assistive Technology

A sighted visitor can read the appointment page and choose a date with a pointer, but a blind visitor may use a screen reader instead. The screen reader relies on information the browser exposes about each control and its relationships rather than on the visual layout. The user hears the information as synthesized speech or reads it on a refreshable braille display. When the browser exposes a date control as a button named “Choose date,” the user can identify and operate it. If the control has no meaningful label, the blind user may hear only “button” and still not know what it does.

A screen reader is one kind of assistive technology, which is hardware or software that helps a disabled person interact with a computer. Assistive technology works with the browser rather than replacing it. The website supplies content and meaning, the browser translates the page's information into platform accessibility data, and the assistive technology presents or operates the result for the user.

Zoom and Reflow

A person with low vision may still use the visual page but enlarge it substantially. At high zoom, a wide appointment form has less room for each field. A responsive layout can stack the fields in one column, allowing the user to read and complete the form by scrolling down. A fixed layout may preserve its desktop width and force the user to pan left and right for every line. Both versions contain identical words, but only one lets the browser adapt their presentation to the user's needs.

Movement and Input Methods

The calendar can create a different barrier for a person with a hand tremor. The dates may be visible and correctly labelled, yet too small to select without hitting a neighboring day. Increasing the target size reduces the precision the person needs without changing the calendar's purpose. Different people may therefore need different interface changes, while the goal is access to the appointment service.

People who cannot use a pointer depend on controls that support other input methods. A person who cannot use a mouse may press Tab to move through controls and Enter or Space to activate them. A switch device can move through the control sequence one item at a time. Voice-control software may activate the “Choose date” button when the user speaks the visible wording. People using a keyboard, switch device, or voice control must be able to find and activate each control without precise pointer movement.

Access Throughout the Task

Some barriers come from instructions, timing, or feedback rather than from the controls. A deaf user cannot follow spoken instructions in an appointment video unless the dialogue and meaningful sounds are available as captions. A person who needs more time to read may lose their work if the form expires without warning. Accessibility must therefore be considered across the whole task. Reaching a form is not enough when its instructions, timing, or final confirmation still depend on an ability the user does not have.

A person with a cognitive or neurological disability may encounter a barrier when the booking steps change unexpectedly or instructions depend on remembering information from an earlier page. Clear instructions and a consistent sequence reduce that demand without changing the task.

An accessibility feature can also help in other circumstances without changing the reason it is required. Captions are necessary when a deaf visitor needs information from a video, but they also help a hearing visitor who cannot play audio in a quiet room. The second use is a useful consequence, but the requirement exists because the deaf visitor would otherwise be excluded from the information.

The booking page can preserve the same task while changing how status, focus, and field purpose are presented. The comparison shows visible changes that support different ways of perceiving and operating the interface.

Not accessible

Only color is used to communicate availability and selection. The email field uses placeholder text instead of a persistent label. Once the user types, the placeholder disappears, so the field no longer shows what the value represents.

Accessible

Dots identify available dates and a checkmark marks the selected date without changing its size. A separate outline shows keyboard focus, and the email label stays visible after a value is entered.

WCAG and Its Four Principles

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines organize accessibility requirements around four conditions that must all hold for a person to use web content.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are a W3C standard for making websites and applications more accessible to disabled people. WCAG gives designers, developers, evaluators, and organizations a shared way to describe the result that a page must provide. Its requirements are generally written as outcomes rather than instructions for one framework or browser, so different implementations can be evaluated against a common expectation.

Four principles were identified because an interface can block a user at different points during one task. Fixing one kind of failure does not compensate for another. WCAG calls the principles Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, and Robust. Their initials form POUR. Evaluators can use the principles to follow the whole path from receiving information to completing an action.

An evaluator uses the first three principles to consider the person's direct encounter with the interface. Perceivable concerns whether the necessary information is available in a form the person can use. Operable concerns whether the person can perform every required action. Understandable concerns whether the content, controls, and responses make the task clear. An interface can make information perceivable and still prevent the person from acting on it, so each principle must be considered separately.

Under Robust, the evaluator examines the programmatic representation behind the person's experience. Browsers and assistive technologies need accurate information about the page's structure, controls, and changing conditions. If software receives the wrong information, an accessible visual design or keyboard interaction may still be presented incorrectly to someone using assistive technology.

An accessible task depends on all four principles. The user must receive the necessary information, carry out each action, and understand what happens. The browser must also describe the interface accurately to assistive technology.

Perceivable Content

Information must be available even when a user cannot see, hear, or display it in the form the author expected.

Perceivable concerns whether users can obtain the information they need from the page. Authors must provide an alternative whenever information depends on a particular sense, color distinction, or visual arrangement. For example, the author must consider why an image is present before choosing its alternative text. A photograph of the clinic entrance needs alternative text if it shows information the visitor needs, such as the location of a step-free entrance. A portrait beside a doctor's name may need an empty alternative when it adds no information beyond the nearby text. A decorative pattern behind the appointment form should also have an empty alternative because announcing it adds no information.

Timing also matters when instructions are recorded. With captions, the user receives dialogue and descriptions of relevant sounds in sync with the video. In a transcript, the spoken content is available as a separate document that the user can search and read at their own pace. When an action is demonstrated without being described in the soundtrack, the missing visual information can be included in audio description. The appropriate alternative depends on which part of the original experience the user needs.

Status information must be available without color. If green dates are available and gray dates are unavailable, the user has to infer their status from color. Text labels such as “Available” and “Unavailable” make the distinction explicit. Color can still help people scan the calendar, but the information must not depend on color alone.

The user must also be able to change the presentation. Increasing text size leaves every line with less horizontal space. In a reflowing layout, the appointment fields move below one another and stay visible. A fixed desktop canvas instead forces the user to pan across every line and may place the confirmation button outside the viewport. With reflow, the user can continue reading while the presentation changes.

Fixed-width layout

The desktop form is wider than the viewport. Fields and the confirmation action continue off-screen, so reading the workflow requires horizontal panning.

Reflowing layout

The content forms one vertical reading path. Every field and action fits the viewport width, so the user only needs to scroll down.

Reflow changes the arrangement. The narrow viewport represents the space available at high zoom or on a small screen.

Relationships must be preserved along with individual pieces of information. A sighted user may associate a heading with the content beneath it and perceive date choices inside a border as one group. Those relationships must also be available when software presents the content without its visual layout. HTML exposes structural relationships to the browser, as covered under Robust.

Operable Interfaces

People need to reach, identify, and activate functionality through the input methods they use.

Operable concerns whether users can complete every action with the input methods available to them. They must be able to move through the interface and activate its controls without being required to use a mouse, make precise movements, or respond within a short time.

A keyboard user moves through interactive elements by moving focus. Focus is the browser's current destination for the next keyboard action. In the booking form, Tab can move from the date choices to a time choice, then to the confirmation email field and the “Continue to details” button. Arrow keys move between radio buttons within the date group. Enter or Space activates the control that currently has focus.

The order of interactive elements in the HTML should match the order in which the user completes the task. The browser then moves keyboard focus through those elements in document order. Authors can override the tab sequence with positive tabindex values, but the manually defined order can become incorrect when fields are added, removed, or rearranged. A logical HTML order usually removes the need for positive tabindex values.

A sighted keyboard user must be able to see the focus path. If the focused time differs only through a subtle background change, the user may not know which appointment Enter will select. A visible focus indicator marks the current control. Sticky headers and dialogs must leave the indicator uncovered, because a correctly focused control is still unusable when the user cannot locate it.

A pointer user needs enough target size and spacing to accommodate imperfect movement. Three small icon buttons placed close together make it easy to select the wrong action. Enlarging the clickable area or adding space changes how precise the movement must be. The interface becomes easier to operate without changing what the actions do.

Pointer and voice interaction both depend on visible wording. Voice-control software uses the words that the browser associates with a control. If a button displays “Book appointment” but the browser identifies it only as “Confirm,” speaking the visible words may not activate it. This identifying text is called the control's accessible name. The browser's process for obtaining an accessible name is covered later.

Time limits can make an otherwise operable form fail near the end. A person who reads slowly may reach confirmation after the session has expired. A warning can let the person extend the session and preserve their work. The extension control must itself be operable. Otherwise, the person learns that the session is about to expire but cannot prevent it.

Motion creates a separate risk. Automatically moving content can make reading difficult, while flashing content can cause seizures. An interface should pause movement that interferes with use and avoid unsafe flashes. When animation is decorative rather than necessary to understand the change, respecting the user's reduced-motion preference removes motion without removing information.

Understandable Workflows

A control is still a barrier when people cannot determine what it does or recover from a mistake.

Understandable concerns whether users can follow the workflow and make informed choices as they move through it. They need clear instructions, predictable behavior, and enough information to prevent or correct mistakes. For example, if a button advances the first step of the appointment flow, a button at the same place should not cancel a later step. When navigation stays in a consistent place, the user retains a stable point of reference. When controls and navigation behave consistently, users do not have to relearn how the interface works at each step.

Users should know what an interaction will do before it changes the page or submits information. Moving focus into a clinic selector should not submit the form or open a new page. Those changes are difficult to anticipate because focus normally means that a control is ready for input. A separate activation lets the user review the selection and decide when to continue.

Before entering a value, the user can read a form label to learn what information belongs in the field. Instructions can provide constraints that do not fit in the label, such as which dates are available for online booking. Providing the constraint before submission helps the user avoid an error rather than learn the rule only after failing it.

When validation fails, the user needs enough information to take the next action. “Invalid” reports a failure without explaining it. “Choose a date after 3 August” states the rule that the entered date broke. Associating the message with the field ensures that software can present the explanation when the user returns to the problem.

A user can finish only when every step in the workflow is accessible. An accessible sign-in form does not help if the following one-time-code step expires before the code can be entered. The user may encounter another barrier when recovery depends on a puzzle they cannot perceive. Evaluation must therefore follow the user's path, including the points where the task fails and must be resumed.

Robust Interfaces

The browser must receive enough accurate information to present the interface through accessibility software.

From the person's side of a task, the first three principles concern information, interaction, and understanding. Robust concerns the software path between the page and the person's tools. A control may look like a date, respond to a pointer, and show a checkmark when selected. But if the browser receives only generic elements and visual styling, it cannot reliably tell assistive technology what the control is called, what kind of control it is, or whether it is selected.

HTML gives the browser much of this information. Authors sometimes need to add accessibility information for custom behavior that HTML cannot express by itself. Comparing those authoring choices first requires an understanding of what the browser puts in the accessibility tree and why visually similar elements can have different representations.

The Accessibility Tree

The browser converts HTML into a representation that accessibility software can inspect.

When the browser loads the appointment page, it parses the HTML into the document object model (DOM). The DOM records that a label belongs to an input and that a button contains the words “Book appointment.” CSS can then place those objects in a grid and give the button its color. The DOM still keeps the document structure even when the visual arrangement changes.

The browser also creates an accessibility tree. This second representation stores the information that accessibility software needs. The booking button becomes an object that a screen reader can identify as a button labeled “Book appointment.” A wrapper used only to create the visual grid may contribute nothing of its own. Its child controls are still available, so the accessibility tree is derived from the DOM rather than copied from it.

It is not possible to establish accessibility meaning from appearance because a screen reader does not infer an element's purpose from its styling. The object's type must be encoded by an element or attribute before the browser can translate its meaning through the operating system's accessibility API for assistive technology.

Semantic HTML

HTML elements communicate structure and behavior before CSS determines how the page looks.

A semantic element carries meaning about the purpose of its content or its relationship to the surrounding document. Authors choose the element for its meaning rather than for the layout CSS will create. The page-structure elements used most often are:

  • <header> contains introductory content for a page or for the article or section that contains it. A header directly inside <body> commonly contains the site identity and site-wide controls.
  • <nav> encloses a major group of links used to move through a site or page.
  • <main> contains the page's primary content.
  • <article> contains a composition that can stand on its own, such as a technical article or a forum post.
  • <section> groups a distinct part of a document. A section normally has a heading that identifies its subject.
  • <aside> contains related material that is separate from the main argument, such as related links or background information.
  • <footer> contains closing information about the page or about the nearest article or section that contains it.

A <div> and a <span> do not describe such a relationship. They are useful hooks for layout, styling, or scripting when no semantic element fits. Giving a <div> a CSS class named "navigation" helps the developer, but the browser does not use the class name to infer that the element contains navigation.

article-page.html HTML 
<header>Site identity and account controls</header>
<nav>Primary navigation links</nav>
<main>
  <article>
    <h1>Keyboard testing</h1>
    <section>
      <h2>Testing a workflow</h2>
      ...
    </section>
  </article>
  <aside>Related articles</aside>
</main>
<footer>Copyright and policies</footer>

Some semantic elements also provide interaction. A native <button> can receive keyboard focus and responds to Enter and Space. The browser exposes it as a button and uses its text to identify the control in the accessibility tree. A <div> styled to look identical does not receive the built-in behavior. Recreating the appearance therefore does not recreate the control.

Using native HTML does not remove the need for accurate wording. The text of a <button> must state the action and a heading must identify the subject of its section. The browser can identify each element's type, but the wording gives it meaning specific to the page.

Accessible Names

A visible label explains a control's purpose and can also provide the name exposed to accessibility software.

Suppose the booking form contains two text fields, one for the patient's email and one for a booking reference. Every user needs to know which value belongs in each field. Visible labels such as “Email address” and “Booking reference” provide this information before typing and stay visible afterward.

When a <label> is associated with a field in HTML, the browser uses its text as the field's accessible name. The accessible name is the identity exposed to software such as a screen reader or voice-control tool. A screen reader can announce “Email address,” and a voice-control user can use the visible wording to select the field.

Placeholder text is different. It appears inside an empty field and may seem to serve as a label, but it disappears when the user enters a value. Without a separate label, the user then sees the value but no explanation of what the field is for.

In the accessible version, the author connects the visible words to the field with matching for and id values. The browser can then use “Email address” as the field's accessible name.

booking-form.html HTML 
<label for="booking-email">Email address</label>
<input id="booking-email" name="email" type="email">

Different elements obtain their accessible names from different parts of the HTML. The words inside a button normally name the button. A form field normally gets its name from an associated <label>. In both cases, the wording is visible and available to people who do not use assistive technology. An icon-only close button has no visible words to provide a useful name, so an aria-label attribute can supply one directly. The reasons for reserving this technique for cases without suitable HTML are covered in the ARIA section.

Roles

A role identifies the kind of object and the interaction a user can expect.

Software uses the name to identify a particular object and the role to determine its type. The booking form contains objects with several roles. The email field is a textbox, the consent control is a checkbox, “Book appointment” is a button, and the privacy-policy destination is a link. Each kind of object supports a different action. A textbox accepts text, a checkbox switches between checked and unchecked, a button performs an action, and a link moves to another location.

Semantic HTML usually gives the browser both the role and the expected behavior. A <button> is exposed as a button, participates in keyboard focus order, and responds to the expected keyboard input. An <input type="checkbox"> provides the checkbox role together with its checked behavior. An <a> with an href provides the link role and navigation behavior.

An explicit ARIA role changes how an element is represented in the accessibility tree without changing browser behavior. A <div role="button"> may be announced as a button, yet it does not automatically become focusable or respond to Enter and Space. The author would have to implement those behaviors. The role therefore sets an expectation that the implementation must fulfill.

Native HTML should provide a role whenever a suitable element exists. An explicit role can describe a custom widget when HTML has no suitable element, but the role must match the widget's behavior and support the appropriate states. Adding role="button" to a <button> is unnecessary, while using a generic element in place of a native button creates work the browser would otherwise handle.

States

A role identifies the kind of object, while a state reports its current condition.

A state is a value that can change during interaction without changing an object's role. When a user selects the consent checkbox, its role is still checkbox, but its checked state changes from false to true. Assistive technology can announce the role and state together, allowing the user to identify the control and determine whether it is selected.

With a native control, the browser updates its state in response to user input and exposes the current value in the accessibility tree. The available dates can therefore be implemented as a group of radio buttons. Choosing one date checks its radio button and clears the previously selected option. CSS can style the associated labels as calendar dates without taking responsibility for the selection behavior.

booking-calendar.html HTML 
<fieldset>
  <legend>Choose a date</legend>

  <input id="date-2026-08-12" name="date"
         type="radio" value="2026-08-12" checked>
  <label for="date-2026-08-12">Wednesday 12 August</label>

  <input id="date-2026-08-14" name="date"
         type="radio" value="2026-08-14">
  <label for="date-2026-08-14">Friday 14 August</label>
</fieldset>

The checked attribute makes 12 August the initial selection. After the user chooses another date, the radio button's checked DOM property contains the current state. CSS can use :checked to draw the visible selection marker from this browser-managed value. The marker, interaction behavior, and accessibility-tree state then stay synchronized.

Developers who build a custom widget must update the interface and the relevant ARIA attribute together whenever an interaction changes its state. If a panel opens while aria-expanded is still false, assistive technology receives inaccurate information about what is visible. The ARIA attribute must also describe a state supported by the widget's role.

Landmarks

A small set of landmarks gives users direct access to the major areas of a page.

A landmark is a role for a major area of a page. Screen readers can present the available landmarks and let a user move between them without reading all the intervening content. On the clinic site, <nav> provides a navigation landmark and <main> provides the main landmark. A user can therefore move directly to the appointment content instead of listening through the site navigation on every page.

Landmarks from HTML

Semantic HTML usually supplies landmark roles without an explicit role attribute. The common mappings describe the purpose of each major area.

  • <main> provides the main landmark for the page's primary content.
  • <nav> provides a navigation landmark for a major group of navigation links.
  • <search> provides a search landmark for site or page search.
  • <aside> provides a complementary landmark for supporting content.
  • A page-level <header> provides the banner landmark, and a page-level <footer> provides the contentinfo landmark.

Naming Repeated Landmarks

A landmark often needs an accessible name when its role alone cannot distinguish it from another landmark. If a page has navigation areas for primary links and footer links, the names “Primary” and “Footer” let a user choose between them. The word “navigation” does not need to be repeated in either name because assistive technology already presents the role. A single main landmark usually needs no name.

Named Regions and Forms

A region landmark provides a destination for an important area with no more specific landmark role. For example, a <section> containing the available appointment times becomes a region when the section has an accessible name. The author can provide that name with aria-labelledby pointing to the section's heading. Ordinary sections should not become landmarks. If every small group is exposed as a region, the resulting landmark list makes useful destinations harder to find.

A <form> becomes a form landmark when it has an accessible name. In this example, aria-labelledby uses the visible heading as the name of the booking form. The heading's id connects the elements, so a screen reader can present “Book an appointment” as the landmark's name. This ARIA relationship is covered in more detail next.

booking-page.html HTML 
<main>
  <form aria-labelledby="booking-heading">
    <h1 id="booking-heading">Book an appointment</h1>
    ...
  </form>
</main>

ARIA Fills Semantic Gaps

ARIA adds accessibility information when native HTML cannot express the full meaning or changing state of an interface.

Accessible Rich Internet Applications (ARIA) is a vocabulary of roles, states, and properties that browsers can expose to assistive technology. Authors apply an ARIA role through the role attribute. ARIA states and properties are expressed through attributes whose names begin with aria-, such as aria-invalid and aria-labelledby. The role attribute is therefore part of ARIA even though its name does not have the aria- prefix.

ARIA is most useful when HTML cannot express all the information a custom interface needs to expose. For example, aria-pressed can report the pressed state of a toggle button, while the button's native semantics continue to provide its role and keyboard behavior. ARIA supplements the control in this case instead of replacing its HTML semantics.

Authors can also use ARIA to connect visible content to the object it explains. The available-times section already has a visible heading. With aria-labelledby, the browser can use the heading as the section's accessible name. An icon-only close button has no visible words, so the author can supply its name directly with aria-label="Close available times". A date field may use aria-describedby to point to a longer instruction. The instruction becomes a description that supplements the field's short name rather than replacing it.

ARIA attributes commonly describe names, relationships, and changing states. Native HTML is preferable when it already provides equivalent information and behavior. The following attributes are most common:

  • aria-label supplies an accessible name as an attribute value. It is useful when a control has no suitable visible text, such as an icon-only close button.
  • aria-labelledby takes one or more element IDs and uses the referenced text as the accessible name. It lets an existing visible heading or label name an object.
  • aria-describedby takes one or more element IDs and adds the referenced text as an accessible description. The description supplements the object's name with instructions, constraints, or other supporting information.
  • aria-selected reports whether an option, grid cell, row, or tab is selected. It should be used only on roles that support this state.
  • aria-invalid reports that a field's current value does not satisfy its validation requirements. A separate description or error message still needs to explain how the value can be corrected.
appointment.html HTML 
<label for="appointment-date">Appointment date</label>
<input id="appointment-date" name="date"
       aria-invalid="true"
       aria-describedby="date-error">
<p id="date-error">Choose a date after 3 August.</p>

When validation fails, the user needs both a state and an explanation. In the example, aria-invalid="true" tells assistive technology that the date is invalid and aria-describedby connects the field to the visible error message, which explains how the value can be corrected.

Live regions are used for a different kind of change. With aria-live, assistive technology can announce updated content without moving keyboard focus. A polite live region is appropriate for information such as “5 times available” that can wait until the current announcement finishes. An assertive announcement on the other hand interrupts the current one and should be used for urgent information.

WCAG and Practical Usability

A standard defines outcomes for systematic evaluation, while testing with users measures how well people can complete real tasks.

WCAG success criteria

WCAG expresses its four principles through requirements that can be evaluated. A success criterion states one of those required outcomes without prescribing a particular design. The criterion for visible keyboard focus does not prescribe the clinic's outline color or CSS rule. It requires a result that an evaluator can observe while leaving room for the design to achieve it in context.

W3C publishes Understanding documents and techniques alongside the success criteria. The Understanding document explains why a criterion exists and how it affects users. The techniques describe common example implementations that can satisfy it.

WCAG conformance

Conformance means satisfying all requirements for a stated WCAG level. It applies to complete web pages and complete processes and not just to a control considered in isolation. A labelled and keyboard-operable field can satisfy its own requirements while the application process that contains it still fails because a later step has an inaccessible timeout or third-party challenge. The process of turning WCAG into a product target and maintaining evidence is covered in Accessibility Governance.

Conformance does not guarantee that a workflow is easy to use. The appointment fields may all have programmatically associated labels, yet those labels can still use language that patients do not understand. The focus order may satisfy the criterion while making a frequent booking path unnecessarily long. Disabled participants can reveal how the workflow behaves during real use but one person's experience does not test every success criterion or represent everyone who will use the service. Standards provide systematic coverage. By involving users, a product team can determine whether the conforming pieces work together as a usable service.

A web application takes on more responsibility when it replaces the browser's ordinary page behavior. Updating the appointment view without a page load can leave focus on a control that no longer exists or change content without announcing it. The effects of client-side navigation, streaming, and deferred interactivity on focus and announcements are covered in Accessibility in Rendering Architecture.
