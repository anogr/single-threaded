Web Accessibility

How a web page preserves its meaning and operation when people perceive and control it in different ways.

Andreas Ögren
~30 min read
Last updated August 2026

Consider a person who is blind and wants to book a medical appointment. The clinic's website uses an interactive calendar to show which times can be booked. A sighted visitor can understand the calendar from its layout and color. The blind visitor needs the browser to provide the same purpose and relationships through software that reads the page aloud. If the calendar exposes only a grid of unlabeled controls, the appointment exists visually but cannot be booked through that interface.

The barrier comes from the way the website represents the task, not from the person's inability to see the screen. The same person may book the appointment without difficulty on another site whose controls have meaningful labels and work from a keyboard. Disability and design meet at the interface. A product becomes inaccessible when it requires an ability that a user does not have and provides no equivalent way to complete the task.

Web accessibility is the work of preventing those barriers. On the clinic page, the calendar must retain its meaning when speech replaces the visual layout. The blind visitor needs to hear which dates are available and move through them without seeing their positions. Accessibility preserves a route through the task when the user's way of interacting differs from the one shown in the design.

Not accessible

Only color is used to communicate availability and selection. The email field uses placeholder text instead of a persistent label. Once the user types, the placeholder disappears, so the field no longer shows what the value represents.

Accessible

Dots identify available dates, while a checkmark marks the selected date without changing its size. A separate outline shows keyboard focus, and the email label remains after a value is entered.

The screenshots show visible differences. Testing the page itself is still necessary to determine whether software can identify and operate its controls.

How Disabled People Use the Web

Different ways of perceiving and controlling a page reveal which information and behavior the design must preserve.

The appointment page can be viewed as pixels and operated with a pointer, but that visual presentation is only one way to experience it. A blind user may instead use a screen reader. The screen reader does not infer meaning from the pixels. It asks the browser what each object is and how the objects relate to one another. The response can be spoken aloud or presented on a refreshable braille display. If the browser reports “Choose date” as a button, the user receives both its identity and its purpose. The screen reader cannot recover a label that the page never provided.

A screen reader is one kind of assistive technology, which is hardware or software that helps a disabled person interact with a computer. Assistive technology works with the browser rather than replacing it. The website supplies content and meaning, the browser translates that information into platform accessibility data, and the assistive technology presents or operates the result for the user.

A person with low vision may still use the visual page but enlarge it substantially. At high zoom, a wide appointment form has less room for each field. A responsive layout can stack the fields in one column, allowing the user to read and complete the form by scrolling down. A fixed layout may preserve its desktop width and force the user to pan left and right for every line. Both pages contain the same words, but only one lets the browser adapt their presentation to the user's needs.

The same calendar can create a different barrier for a person with a hand tremor. The dates may be visible and correctly labelled, yet too small to select without hitting a neighboring day. Increasing the target size addresses that person's movement without changing the calendar's purpose. Different disabilities can therefore require different changes to the interface, while the goal remains access to the same appointment service.

Changing the input method reveals a different dependency. A person who cannot use a mouse may press Tab to move through controls and Enter or Space to activate them. A switch device can move through the same sequence one control at a time. Voice-control software may activate the “Confirm appointment” button when the user speaks the visible wording. These methods work differently for the person, but they all depend on controls that the browser can identify and operate without a precise pointer.

Some barriers arise from the way a task communicates rather than from its controls. A deaf user cannot follow spoken instructions in an appointment video unless the dialogue and meaningful sounds are available as captions. A person who needs more time to read may lose their work if the form expires without warning. Accessibility therefore follows the whole task. Reaching a form is not enough when its instructions, timing, or final confirmation still depend on an ability the user does not have.

An accessibility feature can help in other circumstances without losing that focus. Captions are necessary when a deaf visitor needs information from a video. The same captions help a hearing visitor who cannot play audio in a quiet room. The second use is a useful consequence, but the accessibility requirement begins with the person who would otherwise be excluded from the information.

Access matters most when the website stands between a person and something they need. An inaccessible appointment form can prevent someone from arranging healthcare without help. Requiring another person to complete the form removes privacy and independence even if the clinic offers help by telephone. An accessible service lets the person decide when and how to use it on comparable terms.

WCAG and Its Four Principles

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines organize accessibility requirements around four conditions that must all hold for a person to use web content.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are a W3C standard for making websites and applications more accessible to disabled people. WCAG gives designers, developers, evaluators, and organizations a shared way to describe the result that a page must provide. Its requirements are generally written as outcomes rather than instructions for one framework or browser, so different implementations can be evaluated against the same expectation.

The four principles exist because an interface can block a user at different points in the same task. Fixing one kind of failure does not compensate for another. WCAG calls the principles Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, and Robust. Their initials form POUR. Together, the principles let an evaluation follow the whole path from receiving information to completing an action.

The first three principles examine the person's direct encounter with the interface. Perceivable asks whether the necessary information is available in a form the person can use. Operable asks whether the person can perform every required action. Understandable asks whether the content, controls, and responses make the task clear. An interface can make information perceivable and still prevent the person from acting on it, so each principle must be considered separately.

Robust examines the programmatic representation that supports that experience. Browsers and assistive technologies need accurate information about the page's structure, controls, and changing conditions. If software receives the wrong information, an accessible visual design or keyboard interaction may still be presented incorrectly to someone using assistive technology.

The four principles apply together rather than as independent implementation layers. The next three sections examine how people receive information, act on the interface, and understand the workflow. The Robust section then explains how the browser represents the same interface for accessibility software.

Perceivable Content

Information must remain available when a user cannot see, hear, or display it in the form the author expected.

An image alternative is useful only when it preserves the job the image performs. A portrait beside a doctor's name may need the doctor's name as alternative text if the image helps identify that person. A decorative pattern behind the appointment form should have an empty alternative because announcing it adds no information. The decision comes from context rather than from the image file alone.

Recorded instructions create a different problem because their timing can matter. Captions follow the video and identify dialogue and relevant sounds as they occur. A transcript presents the spoken content as a separate document, which can be searched and read at the user's pace. If the video demonstrates an action without describing it in the soundtrack, audio description supplies the missing visual information. Each alternative preserves a different part of the original experience.

Visual status needs to survive when color is unavailable. If green dates are available and gray dates are unavailable, the calendar asks the user to infer meaning from color. Adding “Available” and “Unavailable” gives the distinction a textual form. Color can still help people scan the calendar, but it no longer carries the information alone.

The user must also be able to change the presentation. Increasing text size gives every line less horizontal space. A layout that reflows moves the appointment fields below one another and keeps each field visible. A fixed desktop canvas instead forces the user to pan across every line and may place the confirmation button outside the viewport. Reflow preserves the reading task while allowing the presentation to change.

Fixed-width layout

The desktop form remains wider than the viewport. Fields and the confirmation action continue off-screen, so reading the workflow requires horizontal panning.

Reflowing layout

The same content forms one vertical reading path. Every field and action fits the viewport width, so the user only needs to scroll down.

Reflow changes the arrangement, not the content or task. The narrow viewport represents the space available at high zoom or on a small screen.

Relationships need the same treatment as individual pieces of information. A heading may visually identify the content beneath it, and a border may make several date choices look like one group. Those relationships must remain available when software presents the content without its visual layout. The Robust section explains how HTML preserves that structure for the browser.

Operable Interfaces

People need to reach, identify, and activate functionality through the input methods they use.

A keyboard user moves through interactive elements by moving focus. Focus is the browser's current destination for the next keyboard action. In the booking form, Tab can move from the date choices to a time choice, then to the confirmation email field and the “Continue to details” button. Arrow keys move between radio buttons within the date group. Enter or Space activates the control that currently has focus.

The focus order should follow the order of the task in the HTML. Positive tabindex values can manufacture a different order, but that order becomes fragile when fields are inserted or the layout changes. Standard HTML controls placed in a logical sequence usually give the browser enough information to produce the expected path.

A sighted keyboard user must be able to see that path. If the focused time differs only through a subtle background change, the user may not know which appointment Enter will select. A visible focus indicator marks the current control. Sticky headers and dialogs must leave that indicator uncovered, because a correctly focused control is still unusable when the user cannot locate it.

A pointer user needs a target that tolerates imperfect movement. Three small icon buttons placed close together make it easy to select the wrong action. Enlarging the clickable area or adding space changes how precise the movement must be. The interface becomes easier to operate without changing what the actions do.

Visible wording connects pointer and voice interaction. Voice-control software uses the words that the browser associates with a control. If a button displays “Book appointment” but the browser identifies it only as “Confirm,” speaking the visible words may not activate it. This identifying text is called the control's accessible name. The later section about accessible names explains how the browser obtains it.

Time limits can make an otherwise operable form fail near the end. A person who reads slowly may reach confirmation after the session has expired. A warning that offers more time lets the person preserve their work. The extension control must itself be operable, or the warning only announces that the barrier is about to occur.

Motion creates a separate risk. Automatically moving content can make reading difficult, while flashing content can cause seizures. An interface should pause movement that interferes with use and avoid unsafe flashes. When animation is decorative rather than necessary to understand the change, respecting the user's reduced-motion preference removes motion without removing information.

Understandable Workflows

A control is still a barrier when people cannot determine what it does or recover from a mistake.

A predictable interface lets the user apply what they have already learned. If “Continue” advances the first step of the appointment flow, the same label should not cancel a later step. Navigation that stays in a consistent place also gives the user a stable point of reference. Predictability reduces the amount of interface behavior that must be discovered while completing the task.

The interface should not act before the user has enough information to expect the result. Moving focus into a clinic selector should not submit the form or open a new page. Those changes are difficult to anticipate because focus normally means that a control is ready for input. A separate activation lets the user review the selection and decide when to continue.

A form label explains what a field asks for before the user enters a value. Instructions can then clarify constraints that the label cannot carry, such as which dates are available for online booking. Providing the constraint before submission helps the user avoid an error rather than learn the rule only after failing it.

When validation does fail, the message needs to support the next action. “Invalid” reports a failure without explaining it. “Choose a date after 3 August” states the rule that the entered date broke. Associating that message with the field ensures that software can present the explanation when the user returns to the problem.

The whole workflow determines whether the user can finish. An accessible sign-in form does not help when the following one-time-code step expires before the code can be entered. A recovery route can introduce another barrier if it depends on a puzzle the user cannot perceive. Evaluation must therefore follow the same path a user follows, including the points where the task fails and must be resumed.

Robust Interfaces

The browser must receive enough accurate information to present the interface through accessibility software.

The first three principles describe information, interaction, and understanding from the person's side of a task. Robust addresses the software path between the page and the tools that person uses. A control may look like a date, respond to a pointer, and show a checkmark when selected. If the browser receives only generic elements and visual styling, it cannot reliably tell assistive technology what the control is called, what kind of control it is, or whether it is selected.

HTML gives the browser much of this information. Custom behavior sometimes needs additional accessibility information that HTML cannot express by itself. Before those authoring choices can be compared, the accessibility tree shows what the browser produces from them and why visually similar elements can be represented differently.

The Accessibility Tree

The browser converts HTML into a representation that accessibility software can inspect.

When the browser loads the appointment page, it parses the HTML into the document object model (DOM). The DOM records that a label belongs to an input and that a button contains the words “Book appointment.” CSS can then place those objects in a grid and give the button its color. The DOM still records the document structure even when the visual arrangement changes.

The browser also creates an accessibility tree. This second representation keeps the information that accessibility software needs. The booking button becomes an object that a screen reader can identify as a button labeled “Book appointment.” A wrapper used only to create the visual grid may contribute nothing of its own. Its child controls remain available, so the accessibility tree is derived from the DOM rather than copied from it.

The distinction explains why appearance cannot establish accessibility meaning. A screen reader does not inspect an element's styling and infer from that styling that the element is a button. The browser needs an element or attribute that says what the object is. It then translates that meaning through the operating system's accessibility API, where assistive technology can use it.

Semantic HTML

HTML elements communicate structure and behavior before CSS determines how the page looks.

A semantic element carries meaning about the purpose of its content or its relationship to the surrounding document. The element is chosen from that meaning rather than from the layout that CSS will create. The page-structure elements used most often are:

  • <header> contains introductory content for a page or for the article or section that contains it. A header directly inside <body> commonly contains the site identity and site-wide controls.
  • <nav> encloses a major group of links used to move through a site or page.
  • <main> contains the page's primary content.
  • <article> contains a composition that can stand on its own, such as a technical article or a forum post.
  • <section> groups a distinct part of a document. A section normally has a heading that identifies its subject.
  • <aside> contains related material that is separate from the main argument, such as related links or background information.
  • <footer> contains closing information about the page or about the nearest article or section that contains it.

A <div> and a <span> do not describe such a relationship. They are useful hooks for layout, styling, or scripting when no semantic element fits. Giving a <div> a CSS class named "navigation" helps the developer, but the browser does not use the class name to infer that the element contains navigation.

article-page.html HTML 
<header>Site identity and account controls</header>
<nav>Primary navigation links</nav>
<main>
  <article>
    <h1>Keyboard testing</h1>
    <section>
      <h2>Testing a workflow</h2>
      ...
    </section>
  </article>
  <aside>Related articles</aside>
</main>
<footer>Copyright and policies</footer>

Some semantic elements also provide interaction. A native <button> can receive keyboard focus and responds to Enter and Space. The browser exposes it as a button and uses its text to identify the control in the accessibility tree. A <div> styled to look identical receives none of that behavior. Recreating the appearance therefore does not recreate the control.

Native HTML still needs accurate content. A button supplies button behavior, but its text must explain the action. A heading supplies structure, but its wording must describe the section. HTML provides the contract that software understands, but it is up to the author to provide the meaning specific to the page.

Accessible Names

Controls need identities that remain available when their visual context is not.

Suppose the booking form contains two text fields. A screen reader needs a way to distinguish the field for the patient's email from the field for a booking reference. An accessible name provides that identity inside the accessibility tree. When the email field has an associated <label> containing “Email address,” the browser uses those words as its name.

The visible label is useful beyond screen readers. A voice-control user can say the words shown beside the field, and a person returning to the form can still see what an entered value means. A placeholder is a poor replacement because it disappears after typing.

The accessible version associates the visible words with the field through matching for and id values. The browser can then use “Work email” as the field's accessible name. No ARIA attribute is needed because HTML already expresses the relationship.

booking-form.html HTML 
<label for="booking-email">Work email</label>
<input id="booking-email" name="email" type="email">

Different elements obtain their accessible names from different parts of the HTML. The words inside a button normally name that button. A form field normally gets its name from an associated <label>. In both cases, the wording is visible and remains available to people who do not use assistive technology. An icon-only close button has no visible words to provide a useful name, so an aria-label attribute can supply one directly. The ARIA section explains why this technique should be reserved for cases where suitable HTML does not already provide the name.

Roles

After identifying an object, software also needs to know what kind of object it is.

The name says which object this is, while a role says what kind of object it is. The booking form contains objects with several roles. The email field is a textbox, the consent control is a checkbox, “Book appointment” is a button, and the privacy-policy destination is a link. These distinctions matter because each kind of object supports a different action. A textbox accepts text, a checkbox switches between checked and unchecked, a button performs an action, and a link moves to another location.

Semantic HTML usually supplies the role without an extra attribute. The browser derives textbox from an appropriate <input>, checkbox from <input type="checkbox">, button from <button>, and link from an <a> that has an href. The role is exposed in the accessibility tree, where assistive technology can use it to choose an announcement or interaction. A screen reader might announce “Book appointment, button,” while voice-control software can treat the same object as something that can be activated.

Roles are divided into categories according to the kind of object or relationship they describe. Three common categories show the main distinction between controls, document structure, and navigable areas. A category describes the kind of meaning that a role exposes to assistive technology.

  • Widget roles describe interactive controls, including buttons, checkboxes, links, and textboxes.
  • Document structure roles describe how content is organized. Headings, lists, list items, and tables belong to this category.
  • Landmark roles describe major areas that can be used as navigation destinations. Navigation, main, and region are common examples.

Native HTML should provide these roles whenever possible. A <button> provides the button role, heading elements provide heading roles, and <table> provides the table role. Generic elements with explicit roles should not replace those elements.

States

The same object can acquire conditions that affect how a user can interact with it.

A state describes a condition of an object that can change without turning it into a different kind of object. Each state has a current value, such as checked or not checked. The browser derives that value from the element's HTML attributes and DOM properties and exposes it in the accessibility tree. In the booking calendar, a date remains the same kind of control after the user chooses it. The role supplies the stable type, while the state supplies the relevant condition at that moment.

Some states come from native HTML. The browser exposes whether a native checkbox or radio button is checked, whether an option is selected, and whether a form control is disabled. The dates in this booking form can be implemented as a group of radio buttons because the user chooses one date from several alternatives. CSS can make each associated label look like a calendar date. The radio button still supplies the role and checked state.

booking-calendar.html HTML 
<fieldset>
  <legend>Choose a date</legend>

  <input id="date-2026-08-12" name="date"
         type="radio" value="2026-08-12" checked>
  <label for="date-2026-08-12">Wednesday 12 August</label>

  <input id="date-2026-08-14" name="date"
         type="radio" value="2026-08-14">
  <label for="date-2026-08-14">Friday 14 August</label>
</fieldset>

The checked attribute gives 12 August its initial state. When the user chooses another date, the browser updates which radio button is checked. The CSS that draws the visible checkmark should use that same checked state. The visual marker and the accessibility tree then remain synchronized instead of being updated by separate pieces of application state.

Landmarks

Large pages need structure that supports direct movement between their major areas.

Roles can also describe the large areas of a page. A landmark is a role that assistive technology can use as a navigation destination. On the clinic site, the primary <nav> becomes a navigation landmark and the appointment form sits inside the main landmark provided by <main>. A screen-reader user can move directly to main instead of listening through the site navigation on every page.

Common landmark roles include:

  • navigation identifies a major group of navigation links. The <nav> element provides it.
  • main identifies the page's primary content. The <main> element provides it.
  • form identifies a named collection of form controls when a more specific landmark does not fit. A <form> with an accessible name provides it.
  • search identifies the controls used to search a site or page. The <search> element provides it.
  • region identifies an important named area that does not match one of the more specific landmark roles.

The specific landmark roles tell a user what each area is for. A general region is therefore less useful when the content is navigation, search, or another area with its own role. When the same type of landmark appears more than once, accessible names can distinguish them. A page with primary and footer navigation might name them “Primary” and “Footer.”

The region landmark covers an important area that has no more specific landmark role. A <section> containing the available appointment times becomes a region when it receives an accessible name from its heading. The name lets a landmark list say “Available times, region” instead of presenting an anonymous destination. Ordinary sections should remain ordinary sections. If every small group becomes a region, the long landmark list makes important destinations harder to find.

The booking form becomes a form landmark when it has an accessible name. In this example, aria-labelledby points to the heading that already appears above the fields. The attribute uses the heading's id, so the visible heading and the landmark name come from the same text. The next section explains this ARIA relationship in more detail.

booking-page.html HTML 
<main>
  <form aria-labelledby="booking-heading">
    <h1 id="booking-heading">Book an appointment</h1>
    ...
  </form>
</main>

ARIA Fills Semantic Gaps

ARIA adds accessibility information when native HTML cannot express the full meaning or changing state of an interface.

Accessible Rich Internet Applications (ARIA) is a vocabulary of roles, states, and properties that browsers can expose to assistive technology. HTML applies an ARIA role through the role attribute. ARIA states and properties use attributes whose names begin with aria-, such as aria-invalid and aria-labelledby. The role attribute is therefore part of ARIA even though its name does not have that prefix.

Semantic HTML and ARIA can sometimes give an element the same accessibility meaning, but they reach that result differently. A native <button> has an implicit button role and receives focus and keyboard behavior from the browser. A <div> has no such meaning or behavior. Adding role="button" gives the <div> an explicit ARIA role, but it does not make the element focusable or make it respond to Enter and Space. The native element should be used when its meaning and behavior fit the control.

ARIA can also connect visible content to the object it explains. The available-times section already has a visible heading, so aria-labelledby can use that heading as the section's accessible name. An icon-only close button has no visible words to use, so aria-label="Close available times" can supply its name directly. A date field may use aria-describedby to point to a longer instruction. The instruction becomes a description that supplements the field's short name rather than replacing it.

ARIA attributes commonly describe names, relationships, and changing states. Native HTML remains preferable when it already provides the same information and behavior. The following attributes appear often:

  • aria-label supplies an accessible name as an attribute value. It is useful when a control has no suitable visible text, such as an icon-only close button.
  • aria-labelledby takes one or more element IDs and uses the referenced text as the accessible name. It lets an existing visible heading or label name an object.
  • aria-describedby takes one or more element IDs and adds the referenced text as an accessible description. The description supplements the object's name with instructions, constraints, or other supporting information.
  • aria-selected reports whether an option, grid cell, row, or tab is selected. It should be used only on roles that support this state.
  • aria-invalid reports that a field's current value does not satisfy its validation requirements. A separate description or error message still needs to explain how the value can be corrected.
appointment.html HTML 
<label for="appointment-date">Appointment date</label>
<input id="appointment-date" name="date"
       aria-invalid="true"
       aria-describedby="date-error">
<p id="date-error">Choose a date after 3 August.</p>

A validation error needs both a state and an explanation. In the example, aria-invalid="true" tells assistive technology that the date is invalid. The attribute does not say why. aria-describedby connects the field to the visible error message, which explains how the value can be corrected. The error state and error message solve different parts of the problem.

Live regions cover a different kind of change. aria-live asks assistive technology to announce updated content without moving keyboard focus. A polite live region is appropriate for information such as “5 times available” that can wait until the current announcement finishes. An assertive region interrupts and should be reserved for urgent information. The application still has to provide a useful message, and the announcement does not repair the workflow that produced it.

WCAG and Practical Usability

A standard defines outcomes to evaluate systematically, while practical evaluation shows how well people can complete real tasks.

WCAG moves from its four principles to requirements that can be evaluated. A success criterion states one of those required outcomes without prescribing a particular design. The criterion for visible keyboard focus does not prescribe the clinic's outline color or CSS rule. It requires a result that an evaluator can observe while leaving room for the design to achieve it in context.

W3C publishes Understanding documents and techniques alongside the success criteria. The Understanding document explains why a criterion exists and how it affects users. Techniques show implementations that can satisfy it. They support the requirement but do not replace it, so an implementation can conform without copying one listed technique when it produces the required outcome another way.

Conformance means satisfying all requirements for a stated WCAG level. It applies to complete web pages and complete processes, not to a control considered in isolation. A labelled and keyboard-operable field can satisfy its own requirements while the application process that contains it still fails because a later step has an inaccessible timeout or third-party challenge. Accessibility Governance explains how an organization turns WCAG into a product target and maintains evidence that the target is being met.

Conformance does not guarantee that a workflow is easy to use. The appointment fields may all have programmatically associated labels, yet those labels can still use language that patients do not understand. The focus order may satisfy the criterion while making a frequent booking path unnecessarily long. Evaluation with disabled users shows how the workflow behaves when someone uses it for a real purpose rather than checking one requirement at a time.

User research cannot replace conformance evaluation either. A participant can reveal a barrier in the booking flow, but one person's experience does not test every success criterion or represent everyone who will use the service. Standards provide systematic coverage. User involvement examines whether the conforming pieces work together as a usable service.

A web application takes on more responsibility when it replaces the browser's ordinary page behavior. Updating the appointment view without a page load can leave focus on a control that no longer exists or change content without announcing it. Accessibility in Rendering Architecture explains how client-side navigation, streaming, and deferred interactivity affect that behavior.
