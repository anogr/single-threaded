UX Principles

How usability, structure, and visual hierarchy shape interface decisions and the code that carries them into a working product.

Andreas Ögren
~15 min read
Last updated July 2026

A design file describes an intended experience. Users encounter the implementation, including every state and behavior that the file left unstated.

User experience (UX) covers what happens as someone uses a product. Can they complete the task, understand the result, and trust what the product tells them? The user interface (UI) is the visual and interactive surface through which that experience is delivered. A polished screen can still produce a poor experience when its flow conflicts with the way people understand the task.

Many interface decisions are made twice. A design establishes the intended behavior, and the implementation decides how that behavior survives real input, data, and failure. The file may show a hover state, an error message, and a loading indicator. Code must still provide an alternative to hover on touch devices, preserve the error through state changes, and clear the loading state when a request fails. This article follows those decisions from usability and information architecture into visual hierarchy, tokens, and interaction feedback. Its focus is how to read and implement an existing direction well. Research methods and the wider process of forming that direction appear only where they help explain the result.

Usability Heuristics

Nielsen's ten heuristics remain useful because they describe recurring usability problems without prescribing one visual solution.

Jakob Nielsen published the best-known version of the heuristics in 1994, building on earlier work with Rolf Molich. They are broad rules for evaluating an interface rather than requirements or component patterns. Their wording has since been refined, but the underlying concerns remain familiar: feedback, control, consistency, error handling, and the burden placed on memory.

Five recur especially often during implementation:

HeuristicInterface patternWhat breaks without it
Visibility of system statusLoading indicators, progress bars, save confirmationsUsers repeat an action because they can't tell whether the first attempt worked
User control and freedomUndo, cancel, and back navigation that actually goes backA mistake becomes permanent the instant it's made
Consistency and standardsThe same icon or action means the same thing everywhere in a productEvery screen becomes a decision someone has to work out again
Error prevention and recoveryConfirmation before a destructive action, a clear path back afterwardA destructive action executes with no way to reverse it
Recognition over recallOptions shown directly instead of ones a user has to remember existA task gets abandoned because the option needed was never visible

A style guide can enforce visual consistency without producing consistent behavior. Shortcuts, confirmation steps, and product terms must keep the same meaning wherever they appear. A “Save” action that behaves differently on two screens creates more confusion than a small difference in alignment.

Heuristics guide judgment, they don't replace it

Heuristics still require context. Deleting an account may justify a confirmation because recovery is difficult. Adding an item to a cart usually does not, because the action is easy to reverse and an extra prompt would interrupt a common task.

Progressive disclosure applies the same concern to feature complexity. The interface presents the options needed for the common task and reveals advanced controls on request, perhaps through a settings drawer. Useful defaults make that arrangement work. They let most people finish without opening the advanced view, while preserving access for those who need more control.

Several heuristics overlap with accessibility requirements once they reach code. A red border alone is a weak error signal even for sighted users, because color perception varies. The interface needs a visible text cue, and assistive technology also needs the error exposed programmatically.

Information Architecture

People can only act on content and features they can find, so organization is part of the interface rather than a layer behind it.

Information architecture (IA) determines how content and functionality are grouped, labeled, and connected. The structure should reflect how people approach a task rather than how the underlying system stores its records. A warehouse may group stock by storage conditions, while a shopper looks for a room, an occasion, or a recipient. Navigation copied from the warehouse would expose the organization's model at the expense of the shopper's.

Information architecture often shapes routing, but the two structures do not have to mirror each other mechanically. A product hierarchy may appear in both navigation and URLs because that makes locations predictable. Deep linking extends the idea to a useful state inside an application, such as a filtered list or an open item. Without a stable address for that state, sharing a link or refreshing the page can send someone back to the application's entry point.

Once published, a URL becomes part of the product's public interface. Bookmarks, search results, and external links may depend on it long after the navigation has changed. A route can still move, but the old address should redirect to its replacement instead of becoming a dead end.

Navigation and search support different ways of finding the same material. Navigation helps people browse a structure or follow a known path. Search helps when they know the words but not the location, or when the collection is too large to expose in menus. Products with substantial content often need both, although the balance depends on the task.

The underlying state model has to preserve those navigation promises. Returning to a list without its filters or position makes the browser's Back action feel unreliable, even if the route itself is correct. Client State explains which state belongs in the URL and how to keep it synchronized with in-memory state.

Visual Hierarchy and Perception

A layout communicates structure before anyone reads a single word on it, through nothing more than how its elements are spaced and grouped.

Gestalt principles describe how visual relationships influence what people perceive as a group. In interface design, proximity is one of the most useful. Nearby elements tend to read as related, while a larger gap suggests a new group. Similarity creates another grouping signal through shared shape, color, or treatment. Two actions styled identically will appear to have comparable roles even if their labels describe very different consequences.

The comparison isolates proximity so its effect is easy to see. A real form should not leave the relationship to spacing alone. Visible group names explain the structure, while semantic HTML such as fieldset and legend makes it available to assistive technology.

Eye-tracking research on text-heavy web pages identified a common F-shaped scanning pattern. It describes a behavior that often appears when people scan weakly formatted content. It is not a template for every screen. Strong headings and visible actions can guide attention along a different path. The broader lesson is that people often scan before they read closely, so the hierarchy should reveal the page's structure during that first pass.

Alignment does similar work at a larger scale. Elements sharing an edge read as part of the same system, even across otherwise unrelated components. That's the mechanism behind why a consistent grid makes an interface feel organized rather than merely tidy.

Type, Spacing, and Color as Tokens

A type scale and a spacing scale are Gestalt principles turned into fixed values, which is exactly what makes them portable into code.

A type scale is a limited set of font sizes used across a product. The sizes may follow a ratio, but the purpose is consistency rather than mathematical purity. A heading can then signal the same level of importance on every screen. A spacing scale gives whitespace the same discipline, so a familiar gap continues to communicate the same degree of separation.

Color can reinforce meaning, establish hierarchy, and express a brand. It should not be the only carrier of status. An error can use red alongside an icon and clear text, allowing people to recognize it quickly without making color perception a requirement. A restrained palette also leaves room to distinguish the elements that need attention.

Emphasis cannot come at the expense of legibility. Muted text may look appropriately quiet to one reader and become unreadable to another. Contrast should therefore be checked when color tokens are defined and again in the contexts where those tokens are combined, because a token does not have a contrast ratio on its own.

Design tokens are these values, such as a spacing scale, a type scale, and a color palette, expressed as named variables instead of hardcoded numbers. The same decision then propagates everywhere it's used instead of drifting apart screen by screen.

tokens.ts TS 
// A fixed scale, not a value picked freely per component.
const spacing = {
  xs: '4px',
  sm: '8px',
  md: '16px',
  lg: '24px',
  xl: '32px',
} as const;

type SpacingToken = keyof typeof spacing;

Shadows and elevation follow the same logic on a smaller scale. A handful of fixed shadow values signal depth and layering consistently, the same way a fixed type scale signals text importance consistently. Organizing and scaling these tokens across a real codebase, including tiers of variables, theming, and the tooling that enforces them, is a bigger topic covered in CSS at Scale.

Interaction States and Feedback

A static design file only ever shows a screen in one state, leaving every other state a screen can be in to get built without ever being drawn first.

An interactive component may need default, hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, and error states. Browsers supply parts of that behavior for native controls, including semantics, keyboard operation, and several default states. Product-specific loading and error behavior still has to be designed. A custom widget starts with fewer guarantees and must recreate the relevant interaction model deliberately.

Knowing where these states come from in the first place matters as much as knowing they exist. A design file usually shows one of them, and the rest get inferred, requested, or invented at implementation time. Implementing Figma Designs covers that gap from the Figma side, including where in a file the other states typically live, when they exist at all.

Not every state earns a bespoke treatment

A disabled state often needs only a clear visual treatment and the correct programmatic behavior. Save richer illustration and copy for states in which people need explanation or a next step, such as an empty result or a failed request.

Performance is itself a usability feature, not a separate concern from the states covered here. An interface that responds immediately is often perceived as simpler than an identical interface that pauses before every interaction. That perception, more than raw polish, is the real reason optimistic updates and skeletons matter.

Optimistic updates show the expected result before the server confirms it. If the request fails, the interface restores the previous state and explains what happened. The pattern suits low-risk actions whose failure is easy to reverse, such as liking an item. Destructive operations and decisions controlled by the server usually need confirmation first.

Feedback pattern
Optimistic update

Apply the change immediately and roll back only if the request fails. Right for low-stakes, easily reversible actions like a like button or a reorderable list.

Feedback pattern
Pessimistic update

Wait for confirmation before showing the change. Right for anything destructive, expensive to undo, or dependent on a server-side decision like payment or inventory.

Loading feedback commonly uses either a spinner or a skeleton. A spinner says that work is in progress without describing the result or the remaining time. A skeleton reserves space in the approximate shape of the expected content. When its dimensions match the result, that reserved space can also reduce layout movement as content arrives.

Form validation has a similar timing choice. Submit-time validation reports problems after the person attempts to continue, which avoids interrupting unfinished input. Inline validation can catch a mistake while its context is still visible, but it should wait until the value is meaningfully checkable. Reporting an error while someone is still typing creates noise rather than help.

What an error says matters more than where it's reported. A useful error message explains what happened, why, and what to do next, without exposing internal system details a user can't act on. "Error 500" tells someone nothing they can use. "We couldn't save your changes. Try again later." tells them what failed and what to try, without describing whatever crashed on the server to cause it.

An empty state needs to distinguish absence from failure. A first login, an inbox with no messages, and a search with no matches are different situations even though each produces an empty region. Useful copy explains why nothing appears and offers a relevant next step when one exists.

Loading feedback also needs an exit when work stalls or fails. An indefinite spinner keeps promising progress after the request may already be lost. The application should eventually present a clear failure state and offer recovery when retrying is safe. Similar fallbacks apply when a feature depends on a browser capability or a script that may not load.

Input methods do not expose the same states. Hover is unreliable on touch devices, so information or actions revealed on hover need another path. Screen width creates a separate constraint. Responsive behavior is rarely a proportional shrink. Components may need to wrap, reorder, collapse, or change their level of detail as space changes.

Motion is a feedback channel in its own right, not decoration layered on top of a finished interface. A transition that slides a new screen in from the direction a user just navigated preserves their sense of place in a way an instant cut doesn't. The same principle argues against motion with no informational job to do. An animation that exists only to look polished adds latency to every interaction without telling anyone anything.

Focus deserves particular attention among these states. A visible focus indicator is how someone navigating by keyboard knows where they currently are on a page. Removing it, a common side effect of resetting a browser's default styles, leaves keyboard users with no way to track their own position. The mechanics behind that indicator, and the accessibility tree these states plug into, are covered in Accessibility as an Architecture Decision.

Why UX Decisions Look the Way They Do

Every constraint embedded in a design came from somewhere, even when the reasoning never made it into the file itself.

Usability testing is watching a real person attempt a task with a design and observing where they hesitate, misread something, or fail outright, rather than asking them to describe the design in the abstract. It exists because the people who build an interface are too familiar with it to see it the way a first-time user does.

Analytics data does something usability testing structurally can't. It shows what's happening at scale, across a real population, after a design has already shipped. A drop-off at a specific step in a checkout flow, a button that gets far fewer clicks than expected, and a support ticket pattern that keeps naming the same screen are all signals like this. Any one of them can turn into a redesign brief months after the original decision was made.

Personas are composite profiles built from research and organized around goals or behaviors. They can give a distributed team a shared way to discuss the people a product serves. A persona is not evidence by itself, however. If a decision depends on a preference written into the profile, the underlying interviews, observations, or usage data should support it.

Applying UX Principles During Implementation

The heuristics, structure, and states covered so far compress into a short list worth running against any interface before calling it finished.

The checklist works during implementation and review. It can expose a gap between a design and the code that implements it, or guide a team that has no designer available for every decision. Each question points to observable behavior rather than personal taste.

A short review pass:

  • Can someone tell what just happened, or is there a silent state change with no feedback?
  • Can a mistake be recovered from, or does the wrong click already have permanent consequences?
  • Does navigating to the same kind of content always work the same way?
  • Are related items grouped by spacing, not just by a shared label?
  • Are hover, focus, disabled, loading, and error states implemented where they apply?
  • Does spacing, type, and color come from the token system, or was a one-off value used instead?
  • Is the thing someone came here to do visible, or does it require already knowing it exists?

Answering these questions does not require a subjective judgment about visual taste. It requires checking the states and transitions that people will encounter, including the ones that were absent from the design file.
